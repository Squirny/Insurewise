Inside jobs and warehouse fires: the property risks a fire-only policy won't catch

By the Insurewise team · 7 July 2026 · 10 min read

Two incidents from the last ten days show how differently property risk can show up. At UBS Gold Shop in Pasar Atom, Surabaya, a cleaner covering an employee's lunch break used the opening to steal gold jewellery: no broken lock, no forced door, just a trusted person left alone with stock. In East Jakarta, a warehouse in Cakung caught fire on 30 June and burned for nearly five hours before Damkar brought it under control; a week earlier, a warehouse storing shoe-making materials for PT Yumeida in Deli Serdang, North Sumatra, went up in flames. One loss was quiet and left the building untouched. The other was loud and could shut a business down for weeks. Both are property risks, and both routinely fall outside the cover an Indonesian SME assumes it already has. This guide explains the gap between theft cover and employee dishonesty cover, what a warehouse fire really costs beyond the stock that burns, and includes two calculators to size your own exposure.

What happened, and why it matters

The Surabaya case is deceptively small: a temporary cleaner, filling in during a staff member's lunch break, walked off with gold jewellery from a stall inside one of Indonesia's best-known gold and jewellery markets. It was caught through CCTV and a stock check, not because anyone broke in. The Cakung fire, by contrast, was a five-hour operation for Damkar involving multiple units, in a warehouse packed with combustible material that let the blaze spread fast. The Deli Serdang fire destroyed raw materials for shoe and sandal production at PT Yumeida, with local residents and fire crews battling it through the night. Different mechanisms, same underlying lesson: the everyday risks to a business's premises and stock are broader than fire alone, and broader than what a basic burglary policy assumes a "theft" looks like.

Employee theft and fidelity cover: the loss a burglary policy may not touch

Most theft and burglary cover in Indonesia (typically an extension to a FLEXAS fire policy, or built into a Property All Risks, PAR, programme) is written around forcible and violent entry. That wording exists for a reason: it is how insurers define burglary as distinct from ordinary loss. The problem is that it leaves a specific and common scenario uncovered: a staff member, or someone standing in for one, who is already inside and simply takes something. No door is forced, so no burglary claim exists, no matter how real the loss is.

The cover built for exactly this gap is Fidelity Guarantee insurance, sometimes sold as Employee Dishonesty or crime cover. It responds to financial loss caused by the dishonest or fraudulent acts of employees: theft of stock or cash, embezzlement, forgery and fraudulent manipulation of records. Cover is typically written on a named-employee or blanket basis, and usually requires reasonable proof of the loss (an audit trail, CCTV, a police report) rather than a witnessed break-in. For businesses handling cash, jewellery, electronics or any stock that is easy to convert to money, it is the cover that answers the question "what if it was one of ours, or someone standing in for one of ours?"

This is not a rare, one-off risk. Global research into occupational fraud consistently finds it sitting in the same range year after year, and Indonesian SMEs share the same structural weaknesses that make it easy: informal cash handling, few segregation-of-duties controls, and stock or till access resting with one or two people at a time. These are precisely the conditions that let a fill-in cleaner or a rushed shift handover turn into a loss.

Warehouse and factory fires: why "fire insurance" alone underdelivers

A basic FLEXAS fire policy pays for physical damage to a building and its contents from a defined list of perils. What it usually does not pay for is the business consequence of that damage: the days a warehouse cannot ship, receive or store stock while it is being rebuilt, the replacement cost of racking, machinery and inventory at today's prices, and the knock-on cost of a shipment that misses its window entirely. That is the role of Property All Risks cover paired with business interruption or stock throughput extensions: insurance that responds not just to the fire itself but to the trading days lost around it.

The Cakung and Deli Serdang fires are a reminder that warehouse fires are not rare, dramatic exceptions. They are a recurring, mechanical risk tied overwhelmingly to one cause. Electrical short circuits are, by a wide margin, the leading cause of fires in Indonesia's commercial and industrial buildings, and a warehouse (full of wiring, lighting, machinery and often overloaded circuits feeding forklifts, dryers or cold storage) sits squarely in the highest-risk category. A five-hour blaze like Cakung's does not just destroy what is stored; it typically forces days or weeks of lost throughput while the site is assessed, cleared and repaired, on top of the physical rebuild.

Market note: two lessons from the same fortnight

Market note (July 2026): A gold-shop theft with no forced entry in Surabaya, and two warehouse fires in the same week in Cakung and Deli Serdang, are a reminder that property risk shows up in forms a basic policy may not have been built to catch: one because nothing was "broken into," the other because the cost is mostly what happens after the fire is out.

Three actions follow. First, check whether your property programme includes Fidelity Guarantee or employee dishonesty cover alongside theft and burglary. Most burglary wording will not respond to an inside job, and this is usually a separate policy or endorsement that has to be actively bought. Second, review your warehouse or factory sum insured against today's replacement costs for stock and machinery, and confirm your business interruption indemnity period is long enough to cover a realistic repair timeline, not just the fire itself. Third, pair both types of cover with practical controls insurers reward: dual custody or handover checks for cash and high-value stock, and routine electrical inspections for warehousing and production sites. Both reduce the size and likelihood of a claim, and can improve your terms.

  • Aggravated theft (pencurian dengan pemberatan, or curat) was Indonesia's single most dominant recorded crime type in 2025, with 38,462 cases. That category covers forced break-ins, though it does not capture insider theft like the Surabaya case, which typically falls under embezzlement or breach-of-trust offences instead.
  • Globally, organisations lose an estimated 5% of annual revenue to occupational fraud each year; smaller organisations (under 100 employees) had the second-highest median loss per case at USD 141,000, despite having fewer resources to absorb it (ACFE, Occupational Fraud 2024: A Report to the Nations).
  • Electrical short circuits (korsleting listrik) are Indonesia's leading fire cause by a wide margin: Jakarta's Gulkarmat fire and rescue agency recorded 635 electrical-cause fires out of roughly 842 cases with an identified cause between January and 20 July 2025 alone.
  • Two unrelated warehouse fires, in Cakung, East Jakarta (30 June 2026) and Deli Serdang, North Sumatra (27 June 2026), occurred within the same week, underlining that this is a recurring exposure for warehousing and light industry, not a freak event.

A note on reading these figures: the fraud statistics above are global research (Indonesia-specific occupational fraud data is limited), while the fire and crime figures are Indonesia-specific. Read them the same way regardless: not as a prediction that your business will be hit, but as evidence that both exposures are common enough, and structurally easy enough, that "it won't happen to us" is not a risk strategy. Key takeaways

  • Standard theft and burglary cover is usually written around forcible entry, and typically will not respond to an employee (or a fill-in covering for one) simply taking stock or cash.
  • Fidelity Guarantee (employee dishonesty) cover closes that gap, and is normally a separate policy or endorsement, not an automatic part of a property programme.
  • A basic fire (FLEXAS) policy pays for physical damage; Property All Risks with business interruption or stock throughput extensions pays for the trading days lost around it too.
  • Electrical faults are Indonesia's leading fire cause. Routine inspections plus adequate sum insured and indemnity periods matter as much as the fire cover itself.
