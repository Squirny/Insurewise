Liability

A runaway truck, nine vehicles, four lives: who actually pays when the brakes fail?

By the Insurewise team · 20 July 2026 · 9 min read

Three brake-failure pile-ups in three weeks. On 17 July, a fuso truck loaded with bottled-water gallons lost its brakes on the Medan-Berastagi road at Sibolangit, ploughed through nine vehicles, killed four people and injured eight more. On 2 July in Balikpapan, a fuel tanker with suspected brake failure ran into nine motorcycles at a red light, killing one rider. On 29 June in Bekasi, a wing-box truck hit five motorcycles in front of Unisma, leaving one dead. Each time, the same question surfaces in the comments under the news: the victims' families, who pays them? The honest answer is uncomfortable: usually a state accident fund with fixed, modest benefits, then a long civil claim against a trucking operator that may not have the money. This article walks through what the law promises, what victims actually receive, and how third-party liability insurance (asuransi tanggung gugat) is designed to close exactly this gap.

What the law promises

On paper, Indonesian law is clear about responsibility. Article 234 of the Traffic Law (UU No. 22/2009) makes the driver, the vehicle owner and/or the transport company responsible for losses suffered by passengers and third parties arising from their negligence. The Civil Code adds the civil route: Article 1365 (unlawful act, perbuatan melawan hukum) lets anyone harmed by another's fault claim compensation, and Article 1367 makes an employer liable for damage caused by its employees in the course of their work. A trucking company whose poorly maintained vehicle loses its brakes cannot simply point at the driver: the company itself can be sued.

"Rem blong" is rarely an act of God, either. Investigators routinely trace brake failure back to overloading, skipped maintenance or an inexperienced driver on a descending route, all things a court can read as negligence of the operator, not misfortune. So the legal theory is straightforward: victims and their families can sue the company and, in principle, win.

What victims actually receive

Practice is a different story, and it comes in three layers.

The first layer is Jasa Raharja, the state accident fund financed by the levy every vehicle owner pays with their annual tax. It pays fixed benefits regardless of fault: Rp 50 million to the heirs of a person killed, up to Rp 20 million in medical costs for the injured, up to Rp 50 million for permanent disability, plus small allowances for first aid and ambulance. It is fast (often days) and it is real money. But notice what is missing: property damage. The nine crushed vehicles at Sibolangit, the motorcycles under the tanker in Balikpapan: not one rupiah from Jasa Raharja. And Rp 50 million for a life, while meaningful, does not replace a breadwinner's income.

The second layer is the civil claim against the operator. This is where the theory meets the wall. Court proceedings take years; many small trucking companies operate on thin margins with few attachable assets; and a judgment against an insolvent defendant is just paper. In practice, most cases end in a mediated "kekeluargaan" settlement in which the operator or the driver's family pays what it can, sometimes covering vehicle repairs, often far less than the real loss.

The third layer should be the at-fault party's liability insurance, and this is where Indonesia stands out, in the wrong way. Indonesia is the only ASEAN country where third-party liability (TPL) motor insurance is still voluntary. The legal basis to make it mandatory exists (Law No. 4/2023, UU P2SK, authorises a compulsory TPL programme, and OJK has said implementation was targeted for 2025) but as of mid-2026 the implementing government regulation (PP) has still not been issued, and OJK confirms the programme is waiting on it. Early estimates discussed premiums around Rp 250,000-300,000 per year for cover of roughly Rp 50 million per event. Until that lands, whether a truck that destroys your car carries any insurance at all is pure luck.

How liability insurance would have changed these stories

Now replay Sibolangit with a properly insured operator. A commercial motor fleet policy with a third-party liability extension, or a standalone asuransi tanggung gugat policy, responds on the company's behalf: it pays the bodily-injury compensation and property damage the company is legally liable for, up to the policy limit, and it pays the lawyers. Three things change immediately.

  • Victims get paid from an insurer's balance sheet, not from whatever a small operator has left after the crash. The difference between a Rp 2 billion judgment against an insolvent company and the same judgment against an insured one is the difference between paper and money.
  • The company survives. A single multi-fatality pile-up can generate claims far beyond the net worth of a mid-sized logistics firm. Liability cover turns an extinction-level event into a managed claim, which is why buyers of logistics services increasingly require proof of TPL cover from their vendors before awarding contracts.
  • The dispute gets shorter. Insurers settle documented claims because litigation costs them too; victims with a clear claim against an insured party typically see money in months, not years of court dates.

For a business running vehicles (even two or three delivery vans) the relevant covers are: third-party liability on the motor fleet policy (bodily injury and property damage to others), public liability for operations beyond the vehicles, and employer's liability for the company's own drivers. None of these are exotic products; they are standard lines sold across the Indonesian market today.

Who sells liability cover in Indonesia

Asuransi tanggung gugat is a standard line at every major general insurer in the country. By size, the market leaders are Tugu Insurance (the largest general insurer by assets, around Rp 21.1 trillion as of June 2025), Asuransi Astra Buana (the largest by gross premium, Rp 7.01 trillion in 2024) and Asuransi Sinar Mas (Rp 6.88 trillion). The state-owned pair Jasindo and Askrindo both market dedicated liability products, and international names (MSIG Indonesia, Zurich Asuransi Indonesia, Tokio Marine) write liability alongside their commercial property books. Industry body AAUI already lists liability among the lines with rising claims payments in early 2025, a sign the product is being bought and used, not just sold.

The practical challenge is not finding a seller: it is matching limits, exclusions and price across insurers whose appetites differ widely for fleet risks, hazardous cargo and passenger transport. That comparison is exactly what a broker does. (That is us: Insurewise compares liability terms across these markets for Indonesian SMEs.)

Market note: mandatory TPL is coming, the gap is now

Market note (July 2026): the compulsory TPL programme under UU P2SK remains stalled awaiting its government regulation, which means every vehicle on the road today is only as insured as its owner chose to be. For businesses, waiting for the mandate is the wrong read twice over: the planned compulsory limit (around Rp 50 million) is calibrated for private cars, not for what a loaded truck can do to nine vehicles and twelve people; and courts will not discount your liability just because the PP was late.

Key takeaways

  • Indonesian law already makes the driver, owner and company liable to accident victims (UU LLAJ Art. 234; Civil Code Arts. 1365 & 1367): the problem is collection, not liability.
  • Jasa Raharja pays victims fast but fixed benefits only (Rp 50 million per death, Rp 20 million medical) and nothing for property damage.
  • Indonesia remains the only ASEAN country without mandatory TPL motor cover; the implementing regulation is still pending, so today the at-fault party's insurance is optional.
  • Third-party liability insurance moves victim compensation onto an insurer's balance sheet, keeps the at-fault business solvent, and is sold as a standard line by every major Indonesian general insurer: Tugu, Asuransi Astra, Sinar Mas, Jasindo, Askrindo, MSIG, Zurich, Tokio Marine.
