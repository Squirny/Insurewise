Cargo insurance: what it is, why you need it, and the Strait of Hormuz effect

By the Insurewise team · 2 June 2026 · 7 min read

Every time your goods leave a warehouse, they are exposed to risk — rough seas, rough handling, accidents, theft and now, increasingly, geopolitical disruption. Cargo insurance protects the value of goods while they move by sea, air or land, so a loss in transit does not become a loss to your bottom line. This guide explains what cargo insurance covers, why importers and exporters should never ship without it, and how the 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis is reshaping the cost of moving goods.

What is cargo insurance?

Marine cargo insurance covers physical loss or damage to goods while they are being transported — domestically or internationally, by ship, plane or truck. The level of cover usually follows the standard Institute Cargo Clauses: Clause A is the widest (all-risk), while Clauses B and C cover a defined list of named perils. You can insure a single shipment, or take an annual open cover that automatically protects every shipment without per-trip paperwork.

  • Loss or damage in transit by sea, air and land
  • Import and export shipments, domestic and international
  • Single-transit cover or annual open cover
  • Optional war and strikes clauses for higher-risk routes

Why cargo insurance is a good idea

Carriers' legal liability is strictly limited — if a shipping line loses or damages your goods, the compensation they owe is usually far below the actual value. Cargo insurance closes that gap, letting you recover the full insured value quickly so cash flow and customer commitments are protected. For businesses shipping regularly, an annual open cover is cheaper and removes the risk of forgetting to insure an individual consignment.

The Strait of Hormuz effect on cargo insurance

Market note (June 2026): The Strait of Hormuz — a chokepoint for a large share of the world's oil and container traffic — has been severely disrupted since the US-Iran conflict escalated in early 2026. War-risk insurance premiums for vessels transiting the Gulf have surged from roughly 0.1-0.25% of a ship's value before the crisis to around 2-8% at points, with some peaks higher. Several insurers temporarily withdrew Gulf war-risk cover, traffic dropped sharply, and shipping lines added emergency conflict surcharges of roughly US$1,500-4,000 per container.

Even though Indonesia is far from the Gulf, the effects reach local importers and exporters. Higher war-risk premiums and emergency surcharges raise the landed cost of goods routed through or priced off Middle East shipping lanes, and global freight rates stay elevated while insurers wait for sustained stability. Energy price swings driven by the crisis also feed into fuel surcharges. The practical response is to make sure your cargo cover is adequate and current: confirm your sum insured reflects today's higher goods and freight values, review whether war and strikes clauses are included for affected routes, and budget for surcharges when quoting customers.

If you import or export, talk to a broker about whether your policy includes war and strikes cover and whether your sums insured are still adequate given higher freight and goods values in 2026.

Key takeaways

  • Cargo insurance covers goods in transit by sea, air and land — beyond the carrier's limited liability.
  • Annual open cover is cheaper and automatic for regular shippers.
  • The 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis has sharply raised war-risk premiums and freight surcharges.
  • Review your sum insured and war/strikes clauses, and budget for surcharges.
