Business interruption insurance: what it is, why you need it, and the Java blackout effect

By the Insurewise team · 23 June 2026 · 7 min read

When the lights go out across Java, most people think about a hot, uncomfortable evening. Business owners think about something else: frozen stock, idle production lines, dropped card payments and staff sitting around on full pay. The rolling blackouts now hitting parts of Java — driven by coal supply shortfalls and technical faults in the grid — are a sharp reminder that the biggest threat to your business is often not damage to your building, but the days you cannot trade. This guide explains business interruption and equipment cover, why every Indonesian SME should look at it, and what the 2026 blackouts mean for the way you protect your operations.

What is business interruption insurance?

Business interruption (BI) insurance replaces the income your business loses, and the fixed costs you keep paying, when an insured event stops you from operating normally. It usually sits inside a property programme — built on fire insurance (FLEXAS) and broadened into Property All Risks (PAR) — rather than being bought on its own.

A well-structured property and BI programme for an Indonesian SME can include:

  • Loss of gross profit while you are unable to trade, up to an agreed indemnity period
  • Ongoing fixed costs such as payroll, rent and loan repayments
  • Increased cost of working — for example renting a generator or temporary premises
  • Damage to machinery and electronic equipment, including from power surges
  • Spoilage of refrigerated or perishable stock when cooling fails
  • Public-utilities extensions, which can respond to interruptions in the electricity supply

The detail that catches many businesses out is this: standard BI cover is normally triggered by physical damage to your own property. A simple power cut from the grid may not be covered unless you add a public-utilities or off-premises power extension, and unless the outage lasts longer than the policy's waiting period. That is exactly the gap the Java blackouts are exposing.

Why business interruption cover is a good idea

For most SMEs, the income lost during a shutdown hurts more than the cost of any single broken machine. A manufacturer that loses three days of production, a cold-storage distributor that watches stock spoil, or an F&B outlet that cannot run its kitchen and POS during peak hours can lose tens of millions of rupiah very quickly — while rent and salaries keep falling due.

BI cover turns that unpredictable loss into a manageable annual premium. It keeps cash flowing so you can pay staff and suppliers, protects you from losing customers to competitors who stayed open, and gives you the budget to react — hiring a generator, shifting to temporary premises or paying overtime to catch up. Equipment and electronics cover, meanwhile, deals with the other side of a blackout: the surge when power returns, which is a common cause of damage to compressors, motors, servers and control systems.

The Java blackout effect on your cover

Market note (June 2026): Parts of Java are experiencing rolling blackouts linked to coal supply shortfalls and technical problems in the generation and distribution network. For businesses, the risk is twofold — lost trading hours during the outage, and equipment damage from voltage fluctuations and surges when supply is restored.

There are three practical lessons for Indonesian SMEs. First, check whether your policy actually responds to a grid power failure. Many property policies cover the fire or breakdown that damages your equipment, but exclude a "pure" loss of supply unless you have bought a specific extension — so read your wording or ask your broker before you assume you are covered. Second, review your indemnity period and any waiting period (often the first 24 or 72 hours). Short, repeated outages can fall below the threshold and recover nothing, while a longer disruption can exceed an indemnity period that was set too short. Third, value the equipment realistically: a weak rupiah has pushed up the replacement cost of imported machinery and electronics, so a sum insured set a few years ago may no longer be enough to replace what a surge destroys.

The takeaway is to treat the blackouts as a planning prompt, not just an inconvenience. Pair the right insurance with practical resilience — surge protection, backup power for critical systems, and a clear plan for perishable stock — so a grid problem stays an annoyance rather than a financial event.

Key takeaways

  • Business interruption cover replaces lost income and fixed costs when you cannot trade.
  • Standard BI is usually triggered by physical damage — a grid blackout may need a public-utilities or off-premises power extension.
  • Power surges when supply returns can damage machinery and electronics; make sure equipment cover is in place.
  • Check your indemnity period, waiting period and sum insured — especially with a weaker rupiah raising replacement costs.
