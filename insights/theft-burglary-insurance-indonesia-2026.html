Theft and burglary cover: protecting your premises when fire isn't the only threat

By the Insurewise team · 23 June 2026 · 8 min read

Two recent break-ins say a lot about the everyday risk Indonesian businesses face. In East Lampung, thieves broke into a PLN substation and stripped out the copper cabling, knocking out power to local residents. In Gunung Sindur, Bogor, burglars forced their way into a minimarket and wrecked the ATM. Neither was a fire, a flood or an earthquake — the perils most owners think of first — yet both caused real financial damage. This guide explains theft and burglary cover, why it is the gap many property policies quietly leave open, and how to make sure a break-in does not become your loss to absorb.

What does theft and burglary cover include?

Theft and burglary cover protects your business against the loss of, or damage to, property caused by forced entry, robbery or vandalism. In Indonesia it usually sits as an extension to a fire policy (FLEXAS) or, more comprehensively, inside a Property All Risks (PAR) programme. A well-built property package can respond to:

  • Theft of stock, equipment, fixtures and cash following forcible entry
  • Damage to the building and fittings caused by the break-in itself — broken doors, shutters, locks and walls
  • Vandalism and malicious damage, including wrecked machines such as ATMs or POS terminals
  • Theft of materials like copper cabling, wiring and metal fittings
  • Money cover for cash on premises and in transit, within agreed limits
  • Riot, strike and malicious damage (RSMDCC) as a related extension

Two distinctions matter at claim time. Most burglary cover requires evidence of forcible and violent entry — an opportunistic walk-in theft may not qualify unless you have wider wording. And theft is frequently sub-limited, meaning the policy pays only up to a set amount for stolen property or cash, which can be well below the true value at risk if it is not reviewed.

Why theft cover deserves a closer look

Many Indonesian SMEs buy property insurance mainly for fire, because lenders and landlords ask for it — and stop there. But the day-to-day probability of a break-in, a stripped cable run or a smashed machine is often higher than a catastrophic fire. The two recent cases show why this matters: the cost is rarely just the stolen item. A wrecked ATM or POS terminal is expensive to replace and stops you taking payments; stolen copper cabling means downtime and rewiring; a forced shutter means repairs and, sometimes, days of lost trade.

There is also a rising-risk angle. Metal theft tends to track commodity prices, and copper has been a persistent target — making cabling, electrical fittings and outdoor equipment attractive to organised thieves. For businesses with exposed infrastructure, warehousing or unmanned sites, that is a real and recurring exposure, not a freak event. Proper cover turns each of these incidents from an out-of-pocket shock into a claim, and pairs naturally with practical prevention: CCTV, alarms, grilles and good lighting, which insurers like to see and which can improve your terms.

What the recent break-ins teach business owners

Market note (June 2026): The East Lampung substation copper theft and the Bogor minimarket ATM attack are reminders that crime-related damage is a frequent, practical risk for Indonesian businesses — and that it often falls outside a basic fire-only policy.

Three actions follow. First, check what your current policy actually covers beyond fire: does it include theft, burglary, vandalism and money — and at what sub-limits? Many owners discover the cover is narrower, or the limits lower, than they assumed. Second, match your sums insured and limits to real replacement costs. A weaker rupiah has raised the price of imported equipment and electronics, so a machine destroyed in a break-in may cost more to replace than your policy was set up to pay. Third, treat prevention and insurance as a pair: physical security reduces the chance of a loss and the size of a claim, while cover catches what prevention cannot. An independent broker can compare insurers and find a package that closes the theft gap without overpaying.

Theft in Indonesia: by the numbers

The risk is not anecdotal. Indonesia's official data shows theft sitting firmly at the top of the crime list:

  • Aggravated theft ( pencurian dengan pemberatan , or curat ) was the single most dominant crime type in Indonesia in 2025, with 38,462 recorded cases — the category that covers exactly the kind of forced break-ins seen in Lampung and Bogor.
  • Theft was the most widespread crime in 2024, reported in 29.92% of all villages and subdistricts.
  • Motor-vehicle theft in Jakarta (Polda Metro Jaya) jumped 123.56% year on year over the January–mid-May period, from 471 to 1,053 cases.
  • Lampung — where the recent substation copper theft occurred — ranks among Indonesia's ten highest-crime provinces.
  • Around 35% of all crimes recorded in 2025 were economically motivated, a share that tends to rise when household and business budgets are squeezed.

A note on reading these figures: police-recorded total crime actually edged down nationally in 2024, even as victimisation surveys pointed the other way — so the honest picture is not "everything is rising" but rather that theft remains the most common and most widespread threat to property, and certain categories and cities are clearly worsening. For a business, the relevant question is not the national average but your own exposure: location, stock value, hours unattended and physical security. (Sources: BPS Crime Statistics 2024/2025; Pusiknas Bareskrim Polri.)

Key takeaways

  • Theft, burglary and vandalism are everyday risks that a fire-only policy may not cover.
  • Cover can include stolen stock and cash, break-in damage, and wrecked machines like ATMs and POS terminals.
  • Watch for forcible-entry requirements and sub-limits that cap what you can claim.
  • Review sums insured against today's replacement costs, and pair cover with CCTV, alarms and good security.
