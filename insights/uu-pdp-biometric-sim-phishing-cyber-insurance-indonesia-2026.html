Cyber

Face scans at the SIM counter, fake "CS" in your inbox: what UU PDP and phishing scams mean for your business

By the Insurewise team · 7 July 2026 · 9 min read

Two stories from the same week capture where Indonesia's data and fraud landscape is heading in 2026. On 1 July, facial-recognition verification became mandatory for every new SIM card, matched live against Dukcapil population records under Permenkomdigi No. 7/2026, a rule the government explicitly frames as protection against scam syndicates. Days earlier, Kompas was warning shoppers to watch for phishing scams run through fake "customer service" accounts targeting crypto and e-commerce users. Put together, they show two sides of the same coin: regulators tightening who may collect and match sensitive personal data, at the very moment scammers are getting better at stealing it and impersonating the businesses that hold it. This guide explains what the new biometric rule means for any business that touches customer ID data, how the fake-CS phishing playbook works, and how cyber insurance responds to both the compliance side and the fraud side.

The new rule: biometric verification becomes mandatory

Under Permenkomdigi No. 7/2026, every new SIM card registration since 1 July 2026 requires a live face scan, matched by the telecom operator against facial data held by Dukcapil (Indonesia's civil registry). Manual entry of a NIK and Kartu Keluarga is no longer accepted for new activations, and operators have been instructed to stop manual activation entirely. The stated purpose is to close down the fraudulently registered SIM cards that scam syndicates rely on for everything from fake courier scams to phishing calls.

The rule sits squarely inside telecoms, but its logic reaches further. Facial and other biometric data is classified under Indonesia's Personal Data Protection Law (UU PDP) as "specific personal data," a category that carries stricter consent and security obligations than general personal data. Any business whose operations brush up against identity verification (an outlet counter selling SIM starter packs, a delivery or logistics app running KYC checks, a fintech-adjacent service verifying customers, or simply an HR system that scans ID photos during onboarding) is now handling, or adjacent to, this higher-sensitivity category. That raises the bar for consent language, storage security and breach response, whether or not the business is a telco.

It is also a useful moment to size the cost of getting UU PDP wrong. The law's maximum administrative fine is 2% of a company's annual revenue for a single violation (UU PDP Article 57(3)). For illustration, a company earning Rp 1 trillion a year could face a fine of up to Rp 20 billion from one finding, on top of orders to halt processing or delete data. For an SME, the same 2% ceiling applies proportionally to a much smaller revenue base, but the compliance and notification costs of a breach do not shrink at the same rate.

The other side: fake customer-service phishing scams

Where the SIM rule tries to close one door, criminals are actively working another. The fake-"CS" pattern Kompas flagged works by impersonating a business's own customer service channel (a WhatsApp number, a social media account, a comment reply) to harvest login details, one-time passwords or payment confirmations from real customers, often targeting crypto exchange and e-commerce users who are used to contacting support for account issues. It is social engineering aimed at your customers rather than your staff, and it does not require breaching your systems at all: only a convincing enough copy of your brand and support channel.

The consequence lands on the business either way. Customers who lose money to a scammer posing as your CS team blame your brand, not the criminal, and often expect compensation regardless of fault. If any genuine customer data was exposed or used to make the impersonation convincing, UU PDP obligations can apply even though the breach happened somewhere else in the chain. And every successful impersonation erodes trust in your official channels going forward, which is a harder cost to reverse than any single refund.

How cyber insurance responds to both sides

A cyber policy built for the Indonesian market in 2026 needs to answer to both stories at once, not just the traditional "we got hacked" scenario:

  • Data breach and UU PDP liability cover: regulatory defence costs, notification expenses and third-party claims if personal or biometric data your business holds is exposed or mishandled
  • Incident response: forensics, legal advice and a breach coach to establish quickly what happened and what must be reported
  • Social engineering and funds-transfer fraud: reimbursement when your own staff are deceived into paying out, the scenario covered in more depth in our article on social engineering fraud
  • Crisis communications and reputational harm support: increasingly relevant when a brand-impersonation scam, not a system breach, is what is damaging customer trust
  • Business interruption: lost income if an incident takes your systems or customer channels offline

Two things are worth checking rather than assuming. First, regulatory fines are not always insurable: many policies cover the legal and notification costs of a UU PDP investigation but exclude the administrative fine itself where local law does not permit insuring penalties, so ask specifically what your policy responds to. Second, customer-facing fraud response (help with communications, monitoring for fake accounts impersonating your brand, and support if customers demand compensation) is usually a distinct extension from basic cyber crime cover, not an automatic inclusion.

Market note: two trends converging in 2026

Market note (July 2026): Mandatory biometric SIM verification and a fresh warning about fake-CS phishing scams, in the same week, capture where the pressure is coming from on both sides: tighter rules on handling sensitive personal data, and more convincing criminal impersonation of the businesses that customers trust.

Three actions follow. First, audit whether your business now touches biometric or facial ID data because of KYC checks, delivery verification or HR onboarding, and treat it as UU PDP's stricter "specific personal data" category rather than ordinary customer information. Second, build a basic anti-impersonation routine for customers: publish which channels are genuinely yours, state clearly that your business will never ask for an OTP or password, and monitor periodically for fake accounts using your name. Third, confirm your cyber cover explicitly includes UU PDP regulatory response costs and some form of customer-facing fraud or reputational support, not just protection against your own systems being hacked.
